Romanticism

c. 1780–1860 · Emotion, imagination and the power of nature, from Turner’s storms to Friedrich’s misty mountains.

Romanticism was a reaction against the cool reason of the Enlightenment and the orderly classical style that went with it. Romantic painters valued feeling over rules. They were drawn to wild nature, distant places, heroic struggle, dreams and nightmares, and the sense of being small before something vast, an experience writers of the time called “the sublime.”

In Britain, J. M. W. Turner dissolved storms, sunsets and steam trains into swirling light, while John Constable painted the English countryside with fresh, honest observation. In Germany, Caspar David Friedrich painted lone figures gazing at mountains, seas and moonlit skies. In France, Théodore Géricault and Eugène Delacroix filled huge canvases with shipwrecks, revolutions and exotic scenes, painted in bold color and energetic brushwork.

Romanticism crossed the Atlantic too. The painters of America’s Hudson River School, such as Thomas Cole and Frederic Edwin Church, celebrated the grandeur of the American wilderness.

What to look for

  • Dramatic skies, storms, seas and mountains
  • Small human figures facing vast landscapes
  • Energetic brushwork and intense, glowing color

Key artists

  • Caspar David Friedrich
  • J. M. W. Turner
  • Théodore Géricault
  • Francisco Goya
  • John Constable
  • Eugène Delacroix
  • Thomas Cole
