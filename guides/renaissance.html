Renaissance

c. 1400–1600 · Europe rediscovers the classical world, and painters learn to make flat walls and panels look like windows.

The Renaissance, French for “rebirth,” began in Italy in the early 1400s, when artists and scholars looked back to ancient Greece and Rome for inspiration. Painters studied anatomy, mathematics and the way light falls on objects, and they developed linear perspective, a system of vanishing points that made scenes seem to recede into real space.

Florence was the movement’s first great center, home to Botticelli, Fra Angelico and the young Leonardo da Vinci. By 1500 the High Renaissance had produced Leonardo’s Mona Lisa and Last Supper, Michelangelo’s Sistine Chapel ceiling and Raphael’s serene Madonnas. In Venice, painters such as Titian and Tintoretto favored rich color and loose brushwork. North of the Alps, Netherlandish artists like Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden perfected oil paint and astonishing detail.

Most Renaissance paintings were religious, made for churches or private prayer, but portraits and scenes from classical mythology became increasingly popular as wealthy merchants and princes became patrons.

What to look for

  • Balanced, symmetrical compositions, often built around a central figure
  • Gold leaf and tempera in earlier works; softer oil paint later
  • Madonnas, saints, portraits in profile and scenes from mythology

Key artists

  • Jan van Eyck
  • Leonardo da Vinci
  • Sandro Botticelli
  • Hieronymus Bosch
  • Michelangelo Buonarroti
  • Pieter Bruegel the Elder
  • El Greco
