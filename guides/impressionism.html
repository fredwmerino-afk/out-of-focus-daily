Impressionism

c. 1860–1890 · Monet, Renoir, Degas and friends paint light, color and modern life in Paris.

In the 1860s a group of young painters in Paris grew tired of the official art world, which favored polished history paintings. They wanted to paint the world as they actually saw it: sunlight on water, a crowded boulevard, friends dancing on a Sunday afternoon. When the official Salon kept rejecting their work, they organized their own exhibitions, eight of them between 1874 and 1886.

A critic mocked one of Claude Monet’s paintings, Impression, Sunrise, and called the group “Impressionists.” The name stuck. Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Camille Pissarro, Berthe Morisot and others often painted outdoors, using quick, visible brushstrokes and bright, unmixed colors to capture fleeting effects of light. Edgar Degas preferred indoor scenes of dancers, milliners and cafés, and Édouard Manet, though he never exhibited with the group, inspired them all.

Monet spent his later years painting the same subjects again and again, haystacks, bridges, cathedrals and his water lily pond, to show how light and weather transform them from hour to hour.

What to look for

  • Short, broken brushstrokes you can see up close
  • Bright daylight, colored shadows and shimmering water
  • Modern life: train stations, cafés, parks and the ballet

Key artists

  • Edgar Degas
  • Édouard Manet
  • Claude Monet
  • Paul Cézanne
  • Pierre-Auguste Renoir
  • Gustave Caillebotte
  • Camille Pissarro
